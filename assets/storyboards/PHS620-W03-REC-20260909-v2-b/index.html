Script

Status: Attached
Let's turn to the chain, because this is the check I actually use when a plan looks finished and still feels loose. Think of alignment as one metal chain with five links still fastened: Objective, Evidence, Practice, Feedback, and Access. If one of those opens, later choices cannot quietly carry the load, which is why I start with acceptable evidence for each focal objective before I fall in love with an activity. After that, name the practice that would produce the evidence, then say how the feedback will be used rather than only collected. The fifth link is the one people skip when they are tired: what your modality and access choices do to who can actually perform. Engagement, completion, confidence, and satisfaction can be useful signals, and they are still not automatically evidence of learning, so I try to have learners practice the target performance before I judge it. Two focal objectives is enough, and a tip that saves grief later is to reuse the same IDs across Panels 2 through 5 so the file does not grow a second secret curriculum. If your draft stops at feedback, keep going until Access is fastened, then open Panel 3 with that chain in mind.

Script notes

Caption: A lesson plan is aligned only when the chain runs all the way to access. Labels: Objective; Evidence; Practice; Feedback; Access. Subject: five stations of one continuous chain across a table, the last Access link still fastened. Not five disconnected circles.

Status: Attached
In this section we can stay with the same chain and ask a more everyday question: which room makes that performance possible. The Canvas discussion is titled Modality Is a Design Tradeoff, and that is the title to use when you post. A learning goal sits at the fork, and from there one path is online, one is live, and one is the classroom. None of those rooms is automatically better, cheaper, or more rigorous; each one trades time, presence, the kind of practice you can see, and who can get in. If the path you wanted is blocked, you still owe the objective, not the original room. That dashed walkway is the workaround I look for in a good draft: an equivalent path with an instructional reason, not a shrug and not whatever tool happened to be licensed. On Panel 4, it helps if every phase is marked Asynchronous or Live and you can say why in a sentence a colleague would accept, including one likely barrier and one credible equivalent. When you post, write from that tradeoff rather than from a preference for cameras or for quiet, especially if someone later asks who got left off the path.
